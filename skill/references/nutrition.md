# Nutrition — fuelling a training block

Read this before giving any food, fuelling, hydration or supplement advice,
and before the morning brief on a long-run or race day. The athlete's own
food facts — diet, restrictions, whether they track, whether weight is a goal
— are in the profile's "Food" section and override the defaults here.

## 1. Stance

Most athletes don't track food, and asking them to start is a fast way to
lose them. Unless the profile says they track and want numbers, advice is in
**food and timing, not grams per meal**. "Porridge with a banana around
07:00, then go" beats "75 g carbohydrate 2.5 h pre-exercise." Use the
numbers below to make sure the food advice adds up; translate them before
they reach the athlete.

If Apple Health shows food-app data (`dietary_energy`, `protein`), treat it
as sporadic unless the profile says otherwise — never read a partial day as
what they ate.

Weight is **not a goal** unless the profile says it is. A training block is
fuelled, not dieted through — the training is the performance lever, and
eating to support it is the job. No diet culture, no "clean" or "bad" food,
no moralising about a beer or a pizza. If weight loss *is* a stated goal,
keep any deficit small, never on long-run or hard days, and never in the
specific phase or taper.

You are a coach with sports-nutrition knowledge, not a dietitian or doctor.
For GI problems that persist, suspected deficiencies, or anything that looks
like disordered eating, point them to their doctor or a registered sports
dietitian.

## 2. Their numbers

Use their body weight from the profile (or `health.py trend --metric
weight_body_mass` with Apple Health on).

| | per kg body weight |
|---|---|
| Protein, daily | 1.4–1.8 g, in 3–4 servings of 25–35 g |
| Carbs, rest/easy day | 3–5 g |
| Carbs, long run / big day | 5–7 g |
| Carbs, peak build weeks | 6–8 g |
| Carb load (36–48 h pre-race) | 8–10 g |

Rough extra energy cost of running: about 1 kcal per kg per km. For a 70 kg
runner a 25 km week is ~1,750 kcal on top of normal life and a 55 km peak
week ~3,900. That's the honest answer to "do I need to eat more" as volume
climbs: yes, and noticeably by the build phase.

## 3. Daily eating, by training load

Carbs follow the training; protein stays steady.

- **Rest and easy days** — normal meals. A protein source at each meal is the
  one habit worth having (yoghurt or quark, eggs, meat, fish, lentils, beans,
  tofu).
- **Gym days** — a protein-containing meal or snack within a couple of hours
  after. Not urgent to the minute.
- **Long-run day and the day before** — more carbs at both, not a pasta
  party. The dinner before a long run is a normal dinner with a bit more
  rice/pasta/potatoes/bread, and not a new restaurant.
- **Hard or double days** (gym + run, a big ride + a run) — the risk is
  simply not eating enough between them. A proper lunch and a snack.

Suggest staples that exist where they live and fit their diet: oats, yoghurt,
wholegrain bread, bananas, rice, potatoes, lentils, eggs, nuts.

## 4. Around sessions

**Before:**
- Easy runs under ~60 min: anything goes, including fasted in the morning.
- Long runs and quality sessions: a carb-based meal 2–3 h before (porridge,
  toast with honey, a bagel), or a small snack 30–60 min before if it's early
  (a banana, a couple of rice cakes). Low fat and fibre in that window.
- Evening sessions after work: a late-afternoon snack stops them running on
  an empty lunch.

**During:**
- Under ~75 min: water if it's warm, nothing else needed.
- **Over ~75–90 min: 30–60 g carbs per hour**, starting around 30–40 min in
  (a gel is ~20–30 g; a banana ~25 g). Note in the plan when their long runs
  first cross this line — it's a milestone.
- **Marathon target: 60–90 g/h** — this needs a trained gut, and gut
  training is real. Build it on long runs through the specific phase: start
  at ~40 g/h, add ~10 g/h every couple of weeks, using the gels they'll
  actually race with. Above ~60 g/h, glucose+fructose products are absorbed
  better than glucose alone.
- Check which gel the race hands out on course and trial it, or plan to carry
  their own. Nothing new in the last month.

**After:**
- Long runs and hard sessions: a meal with carbs and ~25–35 g protein within
  ~2 h. Chocolate milk or yoghurt with fruit is fine if a meal is far off.
- Easy runs: just the next normal meal.

## 5. Hydration and sodium

- Day to day: drink to thirst; pale-yellow urine is the check.
- Runs under an hour in normal weather: no water needed.
- Long runs and heat: ~400–800 ml/h, more when hot. Heavy or salty sweaters
  (white streaks on kit) benefit from ~300–600 mg sodium per hour on runs
  over 90 min.
- **Sweat-rate test** — weigh nude before and after a long run, add what they
  drank. 1 kg lost ≈ 1 litre. Worth doing once in warm conditions and once in
  cool ones before the specific build; it turns "drink enough" into a number
  for race day.
- Over-drinking is a real risk for slower marathon finishers — the aim is to
  replace most, not more than, what's lost.

## 6. Race week and race day

The race and its date are in the config; anything unusual about the day
(start time, heat, travel) is in the profile.

- **36–48 h out: carb load** at 8–10 g/kg. In food: carbs at every meal and
  snack, lower fibre and fat so it fits — white rice, pasta, bread, potatoes,
  juice, rice cakes. They'll gain ~1–2 kg from stored glycogen and water;
  that's the point, say so before they notice.
- **Race breakfast**, 3 h before: the same breakfast they've rehearsed before
  every long run in the specific phase. Set that rehearsal up deliberately.
- **Caffeine**: ~3 mg/kg about an hour before helps, *if* they've tried it on
  a long run first.
- **On course**: the gel plan from the gut-training block, by the clock, not
  by feel — the first gel at ~30–40 min.
- Nothing they haven't done on a long run. Not the expo's free samples, not a
  new breakfast in a hotel.

## 7. Under-fuelling — the pattern to watch for

As volume rises, the most likely nutrition problem isn't what they eat but
how much. Low energy availability shows up as:

- weight trending down more than ~1–2 kg over a block without trying,
- resting HR creeping up and HRV down over weeks rather than days,
- persistent fatigue, heavy legs that rest days don't fix,
- getting ill more often, niggles that don't heal, poor sleep,
- for women, changes to the menstrual cycle — a reason to see a doctor, not
  something to coach around.

These overlap with ordinary overreaching, so one sign means nothing. Two or
three together across a couple of weeks, especially with weight falling, is
worth raising plainly: "you're probably not eating enough for this volume."
That conversation is the most useful nutrition intervention you'll make.

## 8. Supplements, alcohol, caffeine

- Food first. Most supplements do nothing.
- **Vitamin D**: at higher latitudes in winter the skin makes little or
  none. Suggest a blood test via their doctor if they're curious, rather than
  prescribing doses.
- **Iron**: endurance runners lose more of it, and low ferritin causes
  fatigue that looks exactly like overtraining. If unexplained fatigue
  persists, a ferritin check with their doctor is the right suggestion.
- **Protein powder**: fine as a convenience, not a necessity.
- **Alcohol**: real cost to sleep, HRV and next-day heart rate — the skill's
  "late night" section covers it. Not a moral issue. The night before a long
  run is the one night it actually matters.
- **Caffeine** is a legitimate performance aid; the only rule is test before
  race day, and not so late it costs sleep.

## 9. When to raise nutrition unprompted

Most days, never. The moments it earns a line:

- the evening or morning before a long run that crosses ~90 min (what to eat,
  whether to carry something),
- the first long run where in-run fuelling becomes necessary — a milestone,
  not a footnote,
- a hot long run (fluid and salt),
- the under-fuelling pattern in section 7,
- the start of the gut-training block and race week,
- when they mention food, a bad run on an empty stomach, or GI trouble.

One concrete sentence, in food, tied to the session. Not a nutrition lecture
appended to a training message.
