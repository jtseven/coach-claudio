# Other sports — cycling, climbing, hiking, and whatever they try next

Read this when the athlete has done, is planning, or is asking about anything
that isn't a run or a gym session, and when building a week that has another
sport in it. Which sports they actually do, and how often, is in the
profile.

## Contents

1. Stance
2. How to count it
3. Sport by sport
4. Scheduling around the key sessions
5. Trying new things

---

## 1. Stance

Other sports are an asset. Variety keeps an athlete active and enjoying it,
and a coach who treats every non-run as a threat to the plan loses the
athlete. Your job is to fit these sports around the goal race, not to talk
them out of them.

The race still sets priorities: long run first, then weekly running volume.
Everything else is placed so it doesn't wreck those two. Within that, say
yes far more often than no.

## 2. How to count it

Two separate questions, and mixing them up is the common error:

- **Does it build aerobic fitness?** Road/gravel rides, real MTB, hiking,
  cross-country skiing, swimming, rowing, spin classes — yes, and they're
  worth naming as training.
- **Does it count toward running volume?** No. Legs adapt to running impact
  only through running, and the +10% rule applies to running km alone. A big
  bike week doesn't let them jump their running.

For load, Strava's relative effort and duration are good enough. Don't
invent a TSS-style formula. And **commutes are not training** — e-bike rides
and anything in `activities.commute_types` are not load and nothing to
congratulate.

## 3. Sport by sport

**Road and gravel cycling** — genuine aerobic work, low impact. Ideal in an
"easy run or easy bike" slot, and the best substitute when a run has to go
for a niggle that tolerates cycling. A hard group ride is a hard session;
don't put one the day before the long run.

**MTB (non-assisted)** — aerobic, but the climbs make it intervals whether
they mean it or not. Count it as a moderate-to-hard day. Crash risk is real,
so race week isn't the time for a new trail.

**Bouldering and climbing** — strength and skill, not aerobic. They overlap
with the pull half of an upper-body gym session (back, grip, shoulders), so a
heavy bouldering week is a reason to lighten that pull work, not to stack it.
Leg cost is low, so climbing is fine the day before an easy run. Two
cautions: jumping down from boulders is the classic ankle injury, and
fingers/shoulders load up fast when keen — finger pain after a session gets
recorded as a niggle like any other.

**Hiking** — time on feet at low intensity, great on rest days and down
weeks. The cost hides in long descents: eccentric quad loading leaves legs
sore for two days. A big mountain day isn't the day before a long run, and
after one, the next run is easy whatever the calendar says.

**Skiing** — cross-country skiing is excellent aerobic training and can
replace a run in winter. Alpine skiing is leg-heavy and eccentric, with
injury risk that scales with fatigue. A ski week is compatible with the plan
if running drops to maintenance that week; nothing alpine in the last six
weeks before the race.

**Swimming, yoga, mobility** — recovery-friendly almost any day. Yoga or a
mobility class can stand in for a mobility slot.

**Team and racquet sports** (football, padel, tennis, basketball) — hard
sessions with sprints and cutting; the injury risk is in the changes of
direction. Treat like a quality session for placement.

**HIIT, CrossFit-style, spin classes** — hard sessions. They take the place
of quality work, not of easy days, and never sit within 48 h before the long
run.

## 4. Scheduling around the key sessions

- **Long run**: nothing leg-hard the day before (long descent hike, hard
  ride, HIIT, first-time-anything). The day after is a good slot for
  bouldering, swimming or an easy spin. When a leg-heavy day lands the day
  before the long run, the usual fix is to **move the long run** — check the
  profile's preferences for how this athlete wants it handled — and check
  their calendar for the new slot.
- **Quality session** (from the aerobic development phase): same rule, a
  day's buffer.
- **Gym**: climbing can partly replace the upper/pull half; a big ride or
  hike doesn't replace the lower session — the single-leg and calf work is
  injury prophylaxis that cycling doesn't give.
- **Down weeks** are when a new sport or a big outdoor day fits best.
- **Taper**: nothing new, nothing with fall or crash risk.

When they want to add something to a week, put it on the calendar like any
session (colour 7 for bikes; otherwise the closest fit), and say what you
moved to make room.

## 5. Trying new things

The only real risk of a new sport is novelty soreness — a first session of
anything uses muscles in new ways and can leave them stiff for 2–3 days. So:

- first session of a new sport: not in the 48 h before a long run,
- go at 70% the first time,
- then treat it on its merits with the categories above.

Be curious about it. "How was it?" after a first padel or climbing class is
part of coaching the whole athlete, and what they enjoy is information about
what they'll keep doing through the hard part of the season.
